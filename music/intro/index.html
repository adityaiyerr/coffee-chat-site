A Life in Music:
An Introduction

Aditya Iyer  ·  October 2026

Music is a major part of my life, and I wanted to dedicate a section of my website to sharing the music I enjoy most. Before I start, here's a bit about how I got here.

❧

My Journey Through Music

✦

I've been learning Indian classical music since I was in first grade, beginning with learning 1Carnatic music on the keyboard. I didn't realize it at the time, but this would be the start of a long journey through the music of India. Motivated by my friends and family, I spent about two years learning and was given the first opportunity to perform on a stage. It was terrifying, but looking back, the drive to just try something and hope to get better outweighed the fear.

By the third grade, I had fallen in love with a different instrument: the 2tabla. I was introduced to it at my temple, where I watched my future first teacher play for 3bhajans every Thursday. I spent about 4 years learning the basics with him, focusing on accompanying bhajans. In ninth grade, I switched Tabla teachers, which opened up a side of 4Hindustani classical music I never knew existed. I joined a group class with around ten other students my age, and the teacher introduced us to a totally new style of tabla, the 5Farukhabad Gharana. Over the next four years, I learned the foundation of tabla in Hindustani classical music, which is very different from playing Tabla for bhajans.

By college, I was still taking Tabla classes (although less frequently), but the pandemic created a unique opportunity. We had a keyboard and 6Harmonium, and any time I walked downstairs, I would sit down at the keyboard and play, even for just a few minutes. My sister, who mainly played the keyboard and piano, taught me a few bhajans and songs to play. Once again, the desire to play bhajans pushed me to improve, first on the keyboard and then on the harmonium. After a few years of playing at home, I had gotten pretty good.

I also had a great influence nearby: a friend who is both deeply knowledgeable and an accomplished Tabla player. Whenever he would come over, we would practice, and he would teach me something new about the harmonium. I attribute a majority of my early progress to him for guiding me on what to listen to, what to practice, and, most importantly, how to think when it comes to Hindustani classical music. Around 2022, I was good enough to play the harmonium (while continuing to play Tabla) at my temple; another great opportunity to exponentially improve my skills.

The biggest catalyst, though, is being blessed with the opportunity to learn from a world-renowned artist, 7Ustad Akhlak Hussain Varsi, a very well-known musician in the 8ghazal scene. Being able to learn from him one-on-one has taken my skills to a new level, and I keep seeing improvements to this day.

Another way to frame it: self-learning the harmonium brought me to level 15, learning from my friend brought me to level 30, playing at the temple for a few years brought me to level 60, and now, having learned from Ustad Akhlak Hussain Varsi for over a year and a half, I'm at level 100, and the scale keeps growing. My dream is to one day accompany ghazals on the harmonium on stage.

Notes
  1. 1. Carnatic music — the classical music tradition of South India, distinguished from the Hindustani (North Indian) tradition. One of the oldest living musical traditions in the world, with a highly codified system of ragas (melodic frameworks), talas (rhythmic cycles), and a vast repertoire of composed works. ↩
  2. 2. Tabla — a pair of hand drums central to Hindustani classical music and widely used across Indian music traditions. The right-hand drum (dayan) is tuned to a specific pitch; the left-hand drum (bayan) produces bass tones. Each drumhead is treated with a black paste called syahi, which gives the tabla its distinctive resonant timbre and enables complex tonal control. ↩
  3. 3. Bhajan — devotional songs and hymns rooted in the Hindu tradition, typically addressed to a deity or expressing longing for the divine. Unlike the formalized structures of classical Indian music, bhajans emphasize emotional directness and communal participation. The tradition spans many centuries and includes composers from across India's major devotional movements. ↩
  4. 4. Hindustani classical music — the North Indian classical tradition, which diverged from its Carnatic counterpart several centuries ago, partly through interaction with Persian and Central Asian musical influences during the Mughal period. Characterized by extensive improvisation within modal frameworks (ragas) and a system of time cycles (talas). ↩
  5. 5. Farukhabad Gharana — one of the principal tabla traditions (gharanas) of North India, originating in Farukhabad, Uttar Pradesh. A gharana (literally "household") is a hereditary school of musical thought: a lineage of teachers and students who share a distinctive style, repertoire, and pedagogical method transmitted entirely through oral tradition. The Farukhabad style is known for its lyrical, melodic compositions. ↩
  6. 6. Harmonium — a free-reed keyboard instrument that produces sound by pushing air through metal reeds using bellows operated by hand. Introduced to India from Europe in the nineteenth century, the harmonium became thoroughly integrated into Indian devotional and classical music, particularly as an accompanying instrument for vocalists. ↩
  7. 7. Ustad — an honorific title in the Hindustani musical tradition (from Persian/Urdu, meaning "master" or "expert") conferred on a musician of exceptional, long-established mastery. The title is not self-declared; it is recognized by the tradition and by one's peers, and carries with it the authority to transmit the tradition to students. The equivalent title in Carnatic music is Vidwan. ↩
  8. 8. Ghazal — a poetic form of Arabic origin, developed extensively in Persian and Urdu literary traditions, and set to music in both Hindustani classical and semi-classical styles. A ghazal consists of couplets (sher) that share a refrain and rhyme scheme but are thematically independent, unified by mood rather than narrative. The recurring themes are love, longing, loss, and mysticism — often functioning simultaneously on the human and divine register. ↩
