ना जाओ सूरत ए हालात

Na Jao Soorat e Haalaat

Written by Nazar Hussain  ·  October 2026

For my first music recommendation, I wanted to open with a 1ghazal — and not merely a beautiful one, but one that demonstrates something I hope to return to throughout this series: the way a raga's emotional architecture and a poet's words can lock together so completely that neither seems to exist without the other. The ghazal is Na Jao Soorat e Haalaat, written by Nazar Hussain, a legendary composer of the form. The lyrics read like pure poetry. The music makes you feel them in your body.

Lyrics ना जाओ सूरत ए हालात
ना जाओ सूरत ए हालात देखते जाओ,
na jao surat ae halat dekhte jao,
Do not leave yet; stay and witness the state of affairs,
है कैसे हिज्र के दिन रात देखते जाओ,
hai kaise hijra ke din raat dekhte jao,
Observe how the days and nights of 2separation unfold.
तुम्हारे याद में आंखें बरस रही हैं मेरी,
tumhare yaad mein aankhen baras rahi hain meri,
My eyes are shedding tears in your memory;
बगैर अब्र के बरसात देखते जाओ,
bagair abr ke barsaat dekhte jao,
Behold a downpour occurring without a cloud in sight.

करो क़ायम दयार ए फिऱाक में कुछ दिन,
karo kayam dayar ae phyarak mein kuch din,
Linger for a while in this realm of separation,
दिलों पे दर्द के असरात देखते जाओ,
dilon pe dard ke asrat dekhte jao,
And witness the toll that pain takes upon the heart.
तुम्हारे हिज्र में जीते हैं कैसे दीवाने,
tumhare hijra mein jeete hain kaise diwane,
See how the lovelorn survive in your absence,
जहां ए ग़म के तिलस्मात देखते जाओ।
jahan ae gham ke tilsmat dekhte jao
And behold the bewitching spells of this world of sorrow.

The 3Raag

✦
Primary Raag
Shahana

This ghazal is set in 4Shahana — a raga that, on first listen, can be mistaken for its close relative 5Bhimpalasi. They share the same notes. But Shahana is played differently: certain phrases are emphasized, certain movements avoided, and the overall emotional gravity shifts from Bhimpalasi's longing into something more regal, more composed in its melancholy.

The name itself tells you everything you need to know. Shahana means "Of Royal Demeanor" — and you can feel that the moment the composition begins. There is a stateliness to the way it moves, a kind of dignified suffering rather than raw grief. The scale does not weep; it holds itself upright.

"Shahana is linked in Medieval texts to 'a Persian melody known as Firodast', and classical poetry concerning it often features descriptions of springtime and monsoons — both suggesting a relief from extreme conditions: a degree of euphoria is, indeed, integral to the psycho-acoustics of the raga…"

That double register — royal bearing and monsoon relief, grief and euphoria — is exactly what this ghazal inhabits. The beloved is being implored to stay and witness the separation they caused. It is not a shout; it is a composed, devastating invitation.

The Shift — Raag Within a Raag
Miyan Ki Malhar

Listen for the moment when the composition reaches the rain couplet above. On tumhare yaad mein aankhen baras rahi hain meri — "My eyes are shedding tears in your memory" — the melodic material shifts. The musician moves into 6Miyan Ki Malhar, the raga most deeply associated with rain, monsoons, and thunderstorms in the Hindustani tradition.

The move is not accidental. In the lines, the tears shed in missing someone become symbolic of the monsoon itself — bagair abr ke barsaat, a downpour without clouds. By invoking Malhar precisely here, the composer lets the music carry the metaphor that the words are reaching toward. The raga does what language alone cannot: it puts rain inside the room.

This is what I mean when I say the intertwining of raga and text creates something you cannot find elsewhere. The poetry is beautiful on the page. The music makes you feel the rain.

A final note before you listen. You will hear the harmonium in this recording. The player is my Guru, Ustad Akhlak Hussain Varsi — the same teacher I mentioned in the introduction, whose lineage connects directly to the great tabla and vocal masters of North India. Watching him accompany — the way he finds the gaps in the vocal line, the way he holds the drone while shaping the melody — is itself a lesson in listening. I hope you hear what I hear.

Notes
  1. 1. Ghazal — a poetic form of Arabic origin, developed extensively in Persian and Urdu literary traditions, set to music in both Hindustani classical and semi-classical styles. Couplets (sher) share a refrain and rhyme scheme but are thematically independent, unified by mood. Recurring themes: love, longing, loss, and mysticism — often functioning simultaneously on the human and divine register. ↩
  2. 2. Hijr (हिज्र) — separation, particularly the pain of being apart from a beloved. One of the central themes of ghazal poetry, hijr operates on both the romantic and the spiritual register: the separation from a lover and the soul's separation from the divine. Its counterpart, visal, is reunion. ↩
  3. 3. Raag (also raga) — a melodic framework in Indian classical music, defined by a specific set of notes, characteristic ascending and descending movements, emphasized tones, and an associated emotional character (rasa). A raag is not a scale or a key but a complete musical personality — specifying not just which notes to use, but how, in what order, and with what feeling. Different ragas are associated with different times of day, seasons, and emotional states. ↩
  4. 4. Shahana — a Hindustani raga whose name means "Of Royal Demeanor." It shares notes with Bhimpalasi but is distinguished by specific melodic phrases and an overall character that blends dignified grief with a quality of euphoria. Medieval texts link it to a Persian melody called Firodast; classical poetry set in Shahana often evokes springtime and monsoon imagery. ↩
  5. 5. Bhimpalasi — a late-afternoon Hindustani raga in the Kafi thaat, known for its mood of deep longing and pathos (shringar and karuna rasa). It is one of the most performed ragas in both vocal and instrumental North Indian classical music. Shahana and Bhimpalasi share the same notes but differ in characteristic melodic movements and emotional emphasis. ↩
  6. 6. Miyan Ki Malhar — one of the most revered ragas in the Malhar family, traditionally attributed to the great 16th-century musician Miyan Tansen at the court of Akbar. It is strongly associated with the monsoon and is said to have the power to bring rain. The raga carries a simultaneous feeling of release and longing — the relief of the first rains and the ache of the storm. ↩
